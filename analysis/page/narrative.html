Kerala's monsoon is breaking into shorter spells

Across 125 years of daily data, Kerala's south-west monsoon has lost about a tenth of its rain, and its long unbroken wet spells have shrunk by more than a quarter. Every season is warmer, with days warming faster than nights.

Four seasons, four different stories

Kerala gets rain in every season, but almost all of it comes in two bursts: the south-west monsoon, known locally as Edavapathi because it arrives in the middle of the month of Edavam, and the north-east monsoon, Thulavarsham, from October. Summer showers before the monsoon are venal mazha.

A recent national analysis by Data For India found that India's pre-monsoon season shows the clearest change, with more rainy days and heavier rain. Kerala does not follow that pattern. Its pre-monsoon shows no significant trend in any indicator. The change here is concentrated in the two monsoons, and it points towards less rain, delivered in shorter runs.

The south-west monsoon: less rain, fewer long wet runs

Monsoon rainfall averaged 1,886 mm a season in 1901–1950 and 1,679 mm in 1991–2025, a fall of 11%. The decline is statistically significant over the full record, at about 22 mm per decade. The wettest decades were the 1920s and 1940s; the driest were the 1980s and 2000s, with partial recovery since.

The most robust change is in persistence. At a typical location, the longest unbroken run of rainy days in the monsoon has fallen from 39 days to 28. This is the one rainfall trend that stays significant whether measured from 1901 or from 1951, and whichever way the indicator is computed. Rainy days have also fallen, from 91 to 85 per season, with 35 of Kerala's 64 grid cells showing a significant decline.

Intensity has not risen on average. The wettest single day of the monsoon averages 101 mm in both periods, and the wettest five days have fallen 6%. Extreme years still stand out: 2019 and 2018 rank second and third for the wettest single day on record, behind only July 1924, the flood remembered in Kerala as the "Great Flood of '99" after its year in the Malayalam calendar.

Where the monsoon is changing

The drying is not uniform. Fewer monsoon rainy days show up across most of the state, but the decline in total monsoon rain is concentrated in fewer cells: 27 of 64 have a significant downward trend. In the north-east monsoon, 17 cells show a significant decline in season totals and none show a significant increase.

The north-east monsoon, pre-monsoon and winter

Thulavarsham (October–December) has lost 6.5% of its rain, from 525 mm to 491 mm, with fewer rainy days and shorter wet spells. The trend in totals is significant over 1901–2025 but not since 1951. One indicator moves the other way: since 1951, the wettest single day of the season has been rising by about 1.5 mm per decade, a trend that holds under both methods. 2021 was the wettest north-east monsoon on record, at 969 mm.

Pre-monsoon rain (March–May) is almost unchanged on average, up 2%, with no significant trend. Year-to-year swings dominate: 1983 brought 108 mm, while 2025 set a record of 820 mm, more than half of it in May as the monsoon arrived early.

Winter (January–February) rain fell by a third, from 41 mm to 28 mm. Nearly all of that decline happened before 1951; the trend since then is flat. Winter rain is a small share of Kerala's annual total.

Temperature: every season warmer, days faster than nights

Daytime maximum temperatures have risen by 0.8°C to 0.9°C in every season between 1951–1980 and 1996–2025, about 0.2°C per decade. Night-time minimums have risen by roughly half as much, 0.4°C to 0.6°C. In the monsoon, the gap between day and night has widened significantly, by 0.5°C. That is consistent with fewer rainy, cloudy days, though this analysis does not test the link.

Hot days have become routine. Using each season's 1961–1990 90th percentile as the threshold, 7% of monsoon days crossed it in 1951–1980. In 1996–2025, 23% did. The north-east monsoon season shows the largest rise, from 4% to 26% of days.

How the method changes the answer

National analyses such as the Data For India article work from area-averaged rainfall: average across the whole region first, then count rainy days, spells and extremes on that single series. The ETCCDI indicators were designed to be computed at individual stations or grid cells. This analysis did both, to see how much the choice matters for one state.

For season totals and average temperatures, the two methods give identical results, because averaging and adding commute. For anything that counts days against a threshold or picks out a maximum, they diverge sharply. Averaged across Kerala first, the monsoon has 115 rainy days and a longest wet spell of 86 days. Measured per location, it has 91 rainy days and a longest wet spell of 39 days. A state-average day counts as rainy if enough of Kerala gets rain, even when most places stay dry. The same averaging dilutes extremes: the wettest monsoon day falls from 101 mm per location to 73 mm on the state series.

The methods mostly agree on the direction of change, but not always on whether it is real. In 9 of 48 tests of frequency and extreme indicators, one method finds a significant trend and the other does not. The pre-monsoon's longest wet spell is the clearest case: the state-average series shows a significant 25% decline; per location, the change is 6.5% and not significant.

Data, method and limits

  • Rainfall: IMD 0.25° daily gridded rainfall, 1901–2025, built from about 6,900 rain gauges (Pai et al., 2014). 64 grid cells overlap Kerala by at least a quarter of their area; each is weighted by its overlap.
  • Temperature: IMD 1° daily gridded maximum and minimum, 1951–2025. Only 9 cells overlap Kerala, several partly over the sea or Tamil Nadu, so temperature results describe the region broadly, not district detail.
  • Indicators: seasonal versions of ETCCDI indices. Rainy day: at least 1 mm. Heavy-rain day: at least 20 mm. Spells are counted within each season.
  • Trends: Sen's slope, with significance from the Hamed–Rao modified Mann–Kendall test, which allows for autocorrelation. With dozens of tests, a few significant results at 5% are expected by chance.
  • Early-record check: gauge coverage was thinner in the early 1900s, which can smooth rainy-day counts and extremes. Every trend was re-run from 1951. Many full-record rainfall trends lose significance from 1951 onward, meaning part of the long-run decline reflects a wet first half of the century. The shortening of monsoon wet spells holds in both windows.
  • Validation: the record five-day rainfall ends on 19 July 1924 and the 2018 peak on 17 August 2018, matching the known dates of both floods.
